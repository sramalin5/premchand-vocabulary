¶1 Pandit Hridaynath was respected in Ayodhya. Not wealthy, he lived comfortably on rent from several houses. Rents had risen, allowing him his own conveyance. Thoughtful, educated and experienced, he lacked practical force despite all he knew. Society was a dreadful ghost to fear continually: displease it and life was unsafe. His wife Jageshwari reflected him, sharing his thoughts and wishes without disagreement. She worshipped Shiva, he Vishnu, but both equally believed in charity and fasting. They were more devout than educated people generally are, perhaps because they had only one daughter. Coverage and translation notes

The changing remedies
Entertainment, religious discipline and teaching are successively encouraged and then restricted. The translation preserves the parents' shifting reasoning and Kailasi's growing objection to control.
The final ambiguity
The father does not name his one remedy. The source stops there, so the English does too. No proposed marriage, death or other solution is inserted.
Narrative and character judgments
Descriptions of widowhood as penance and of despair as moral decline are retained in their narrative or character context. Kailasi's own statements about consciousness and self-respect remain equally explicit.
Names and source irregularities
Kailasi is the affectionate short form of Kailasikumari. Doctor Gaur's proposal is not identified in the source, and no external details are supplied. In ¶68 बातों appears irregular where the surrounding wedding-party image is followed; the Hindi is unchanged.
