¶1 साधु-संतों के सत्संग से बुरे भी अच्छे हो जाते हैं, किन्तु पयाग का दुर्भाग्य था, कि उस पर सत्संग का उल्टा ही असर हुआ। उसे गाँजे, चरस और भंग का चस्का पड़ गया, जिसका फल यह हुआ कि एक मेहनती, उद्यमशील युवक आलस्य का उपासक बन बैठा। जीवन-संग्राम में यह आनन्द कहाँ ! किसी वट-वृक्ष के नीचे धूनी जल रही है, एक जटाधारी महात्मा विराज रहे हैं, भक्तजन उन्हें घेरे बैठे हुए हैं, और तिल-तिल पर चरस के दम लग रहे हैं। बीच-बीच में भजन भी हो जाते हैं। मजूरी-धातूरी में यह स्वर्ग-सुख कहाँ ! चिलम भरना पयाग का काम था। भक्तों को परलोक में पुण्य-फल की आशा थी, पयाग को तत्काल फल मिलता था , चिलमों पर पहला हक उसी का होता 'हाँ-हाँ, कह तो दिया, मेरे पास पैसे नहीं हैं।' 'गहने बनवाने के लिए पैसे हैं और मैं चार पैसे माँगता हूँ, तो यों जवाब देती है !' रुक्मिन तिनक कर बोली —  गहने बनवाती हूँ, तो तुम्हारी छाती क्यों फटती है ? तुमने तो पीतल का छल्ला भी नहीं बनवाया, या इतना भी नहीं देखा जाता ? पयाग उस दिन घर न आया। रात के नौ बज गये, तब रुक्मिन ने किवाड़ बंद कर लिये। समझी, गाँव में कहीं छिपा बैठा होगा। समझता होगा, मुझे मनाने आयेगी, मेरी बला जाती है। जब दूसरे दिन भी पयाग न आया, तो रुक्मिन को चिंता हुई। गाँव भर छान आयी। चिड़िया किसी अव्े पर न मिली। उस दिन उसने रसोई नहीं बनायी। रात को लेटी भी तो बहुत देर तक आँखें न लगीं। शंका हो रही थी, पयाग सचमुच तो विरक्त नहीं हो गया। उसने सोचा, प्रात:काल पत्ता-पत्ता छान डालूँगी, किसी साधु-संत के साथ होगा। जा कर थाने में रपट कर दूँगी। अभी तड़का ही था कि रुक्मिन थाने में चलने को तैयार हो गयी। किवाड़ बंद करके निकली ही थी कि पयाग आता हुआ दिखाई दिया। पर वह अकेला न था। उसके पीछे-पीछे एक स्त्री भी थी। उसकी छींट की साड़ी, रँगी हुई चादर, लम्बा घूँघट और शर्मीली चाल देख कर रुक्मिन का कलेजा धक्-से हो गया। वह एक क्षण हतबुद्धि-सी खड़ी रही, तब बढ़ कर नयी सौत को दोनों हाथों के बीच में ले लिया और उसे इस भाँति धीरे-धीरे घर के अंदर ले चली, जैसे कोई रोगी जीवन से निराश हो कर विष-पान कर रहा हो ! जब पड़ोसियों की भीड़ छँट गयी तो रुक्मिन ने पयाग से पूछा—इसे कहाँ से लाये ? पयाग ने हँस कर कहा —घर से भागी जाती थी, मुझे रास्ते में मिल गयी। घर का काम-धांधा करेगी, पड़ी रहेगी। 'मालूम होता है, मुझसे तुम्हारा जी भर गया।' पयाग ने तिरछी चितवनों से देख कर कहा —दुत् पगली ! इसे तेरी सेवा-टहल करने को लाया हूँ। 'नयी के आगे पुरानी को कौन पूछता है ?' 'चल, मन जिससे मिले वही नयी है, मन जिससे न मिले वही पुरानी है। ला, कुछ पैसा हो तो दे दे, तीन दिन से दम नहीं लगाया, पैर सीधे नहीं पड़ते। हाँ, देख दो-चार दिन इस बेचारी को खिला-पिला दे, फिर तो आप ही काम करने लगेगी।' रुक्मिन ने पूरा रुपया ला कर पयाग के हाथ पर रख दिया। दूसरी बार कहने की जरूरत ही न पड़ी। पयाग में चाहे और कोई गुण हो या न हो, यह मानना पड़ेगा कि वह शासन के मूल सिध्दांतों से परिचित था। उसने भेद-नीति को अपना लक्ष्य बना लिया था। एक मास तक किसी प्रकार की विघ्न-बाधा न पड़ी। रुक्मिन अपनी सारी चौकड़ियाँ भूल गयी थी। बड़े तड़के उठती, कभी लकड़ियाँ तोड़ कर, कभी चारा काट कर, कभी उपले पाथ कर बाजार ले जाती। वहाँ जो कुछ मिलता, उसका आधा तो पयाग के हत्थे चढ़ा देती। आधो में घर का काम चलता। वह सौत को कोई काम न करने देती। पड़ोसियों से कहती , बहन, सौत है तो क्या, है तो अभी कल की बहुरिया। दो-चार महीने भी आराम से न रहेगी, तो क्या याद करेगी। मैं तो काम करने को हूँ ही। गाँव भर में रुक्मिन के शील-स्वभाव का बखान होता था, पर सत्संगी घाघ पयाग सब कुछ समझता था और अपनी नीति की सफलता पर प्रसन्न होता था। एक दिन बहू ने कहा —दीदी, अब तो घर में बैठे-बैठे जी ऊबता है। मुझे भी कोई काम दिला दो। रुक्मिन ने स्नेह सिंचित स्वर में कहा —क्या मेरे मुख में कालिख पुतवाने पर लगी हुई है ? भीतर का काम किये जा, बाहर के लिए मैं हूँ ही। बहू का नाम कौशल्या था, जो बिगड़ कर सिलिया हो गया था। इस वक्त सिलिया ने कुछ जवाब न दिया। लेकिन वह लौंडियों की दशा अब उसके लिए असह्य हो गयी थी। वह दिन भर घर का काम करते-करते मरे, कोई नहीं पूछता। रुक्मिन बाहर से चार पैसे लाती है, तो घर की मालकिन बनी हुई है। अब सिलिया भी मजूरी करेगी और मालकिन का घमंड तोड़ देगी। पयाग पैसों का यार है, यह बात उससे अब छिपी न थी। जब रुक्मिन चारा ले कर बाजार चली गयी, तो उसने घर की टट्टी लगायी और गाँव का रंग-ढंग देखने के लिए निकल पड़ी। गाँव में ब्राह्मण, ठाकुर, कायस्थ, बनिये सभी थे। सिलिया ने शील और संकोच का कुछ ऐसा स्वाँग रचा की सभी स्त्रियाँ उस पर मुग्ध हो गयीं। किसी ने चावल दिया, किसी ने दाल, किसी ने कुछ। नयी बहू की आवभगत कौन न करता ? पहले ही दौरे में सिलिया को मालूम हो गया कि गाँव में पिसनहारी का स्थान खाली है और वह इस कमी को पूरा कर सकती है। वह यहाँ से घर लौटी, तो उसके सिर पर गेहूँ से भरी हुई एक टोकरी थी। पयाग ने पहर रात ही से चक्की की आवाज सुनी, तो रुक्मिन से बोला —  आज तो सिलिया अभी से पीसने लगी। रुक्मिन बाजार से आटा लायी थी। अनाज और आटे के भाव में विशेष अंतर न था। उसे आश्चर्य हुआ कि सिलिया इतने सबेरे क्या पीस रही है। उठ कर कोठरी में आयी, तो देखा कि सिलिया अँधेरे  में बैठी कुछ पीस रही है। उसने जा कर उसका हाथ पकड़ लिया और टोकरी को उठा कर बोली —  तुझसे किसने पीसने को कहा है ? किसका अनाज पीस रही है ? सिलिया ने निश्शंक हो कर कहा —तुम जा कर आराम से सोती क्यों नहीं। मैं पीसती हूँ, तो तुम्हारा क्या बिगड़ता है ! चक्की की घुमुर-घुमुर भी नहीं सही जाती ? लाओ, टोकरी दे दो, बैठे-बैठे कब तक खाऊँगी, दो महीने तो हो गये। 'मैंने तो तुझसे कुछ नहीं कहा !' 'तुम कहो, चाहे न कहो; अपना धरम भी तो कुछ है।' 'तू अभी यहाँ के आदमियों को नहीं जानती। आटा तो पिसाते सबको अच्छा लगता है। पैसे देते रोते हैं। किसका गेहूँ है ? मैं सबेरे उसके सिर पर पटक आऊँगी।' सिलिया ने रुक्मिन के हाथ से टोकरी छीन ली और बोली —  पैसे क्यों न देंगे ? कुछ बेगार करती हूँ ? 'तू न मानेगी ?' 'तुम्हारी लौंडी बन कर न रहूँगी।' यह तकरार सुन कर पयाग भी आ पहुँचा और रुक्मिन से बोला —  काम करती है तो करने क्यों नहीं देती ? अब क्या जनम भर बहुरिया ही बनी रहेगी ? हो गये दो महीने। 'तुम क्या जानो, नाक तो मेरी कटेगी।' सिलिया बोल उठी , तो क्या कोई बैठे खिलाता है ? चौका-बरतन, झाडू-बहारू, रोटी-पानी, पीसना-कूटना, यह कौन करता है ? पानी खींचते-खींचते मेरे हाथों में घट्ठे पड़ गये। मुझसे अब सारा काम न होगा। पयाग ने कहा —तो तू ही बाजार जाएा कर। घर का काम रहने दे ! रुक्मिन कर लेगी। रुक्मिन ने आपत्ति की , ऐसी बात मुँह से निकालते लाज नहीं आती ? तीन दिन की बहुरिया बाजार में घूमेगी, तो संसार क्या कहेगा ! सिलिया ने आग्रह करके कहा —संसार क्या कहेगा, क्या कोई ऐब करने जाती हूँ ? सिलिया की डिग्री हो गयी। आधिपत्य रुक्मिन के हाथ से निकल गया। सिलिया की अमलदारी हो गयी। जवान औरत थी। गेहूँ पीस कर उठी तो औरों के साथ घास छीलने चली गयी, और इतनी घास छीली कि सब दंग रह गयीं ! गट्ठा उठाये न उठता था। जिन पुरुषों को घास छीलने का बड़ा अभ्यास था, उनसे भी उसने बाजी मार ली ! यह गट्ठा बारह आने का बिका। सिलिया ने आटा, चावल, दाल, तेल, नमक, तरकारी, मसाला सब कुछ लिया, और चार आने बचा भी लिये। रुक्मिन ने समझ रखा था कि सिलिया बाजार से दो-चार आने पैसे ले कर लौटेगी तो उसे डाटूँगी और दूसरे दिन से फिर बाजार जाने लगूँगी। फिर मेरा राज्य हो जायगा। पर यह सामान देखे, तो आँखें खुल गयीं। पयाग खाने बैठा तो मसालेदार तरकारी का बखान करने लगा। महीनों से ऐसी स्वादिष्ट वस्तु मयस्सर न हुई थी। बहुत प्रसन्न हुआ। भोजन करके वह बाहर जाने लगा, तो सिलिया बरोठे में खड़ी मिल गयी। बोला —  आज कितने पैसे मिले ? One day the young wife said, ‘Elder sister, I'm tired of sitting at home. Find me some work too.’ In an affectionate voice Rukmin said, ‘Are you bent on blackening my face? Keep doing the indoor work; I'm here for the outside.’ The young wife's name was Kaushalya, corrupted into Siliya. She made no reply then, but this servant's condition had become unbearable. She could work herself to death indoors all day and no one cared, while Rukmin brought in a few coins and ruled the house. Now Siliya too would earn wages and break the mistress's pride. She knew by now that Payag loved money. When Rukmin went to market with fodder, she closed the reed door and went to inspect the village. Brahmins, Thakurs, Kayasths and merchants all lived there. Siliya put on such a show of modesty and reserve that every woman was charmed. One gave rice, another lentils, another something else. Who would not welcome a new bride? On this first round she learned the village had no woman grinding grain for wages and she could fill the gap. She returned home with a basket full of wheat on her head. Hearing the hand mill hours before dawn, Payag said to Rukmin, ‘Siliya has started grinding already today.’ Rukmin had bought flour at market; grain and flour differed little in price. Wondering what Siliya was grinding so early, she rose and went into the room. Siliya sat grinding in the dark. Rukmin caught her hand, lifted the basket and said, ‘Who told you to grind? Whose grain is this?’ Fearlessly Siliya answered, ‘Why don't you go and sleep comfortably? What harm does my grinding do you? Can't you bear even the mill's rumbling? Give me the basket. How long am I to eat sitting idle? It's been two months.’ ‘I haven't said anything to you!’ ‘Whether you say anything or not, I have some duty of my own.’ ‘You don't know the people here yet. Everyone likes having flour ground, but they cry when it comes to paying. Whose wheat is it? I'll throw it back at their heads in the morning.’ Siliya snatched the basket from Rukmin and said, ‘Why shouldn't they pay? Am I doing forced unpaid labor?’ ‘You won't listen?’ ‘I won't live as your slave.’ Hearing the quarrel, Payag came too. ‘If she's working, why won't you let her? Must she remain a new bride all her life? It's been two months.’ ‘What do you know? It's my nose that will be cut off.’ Siliya burst out, ‘Does anyone feed me while I sit idle? Who does the cooking and dishes, sweeping, bread and water, grinding and pounding? Drawing water has calloused my hands. I can't do everything now.’ Payag said, ‘Then you go to market. Leave the housework; Rukmin will do it.’ Rukmin protested, ‘Aren't you ashamed to say that? A bride of three days roaming the market—what will the world say?’ Siliya insisted, ‘What will it say? Am I going to do anything shameful?’ Judgment was given for Siliya. Authority passed from Rukmin's hands, and Siliya's rule began. She was young. Finishing the wheat, she went with the others to cut grass and cut so much that everyone was astonished. The bundle was almost too heavy to lift. She surpassed even men long practiced at grass cutting. It sold for twelve annas. She bought flour, rice, lentils, oil, salt, vegetables and spices, and still saved four annas. Rukmin had thought that when Siliya returned with only a few annas she would scold her and resume going to market next day; her own rule would return. But the sight of these provisions opened her eyes. Sitting down to eat, Payag praised the spiced vegetables. He hadn't had anything so tasty for months and was delighted. As he went out after eating, he found Siliya in the vestibule. ‘How much did you get today?’ ‘Twelve annas.’ ‘Spent it all? Give me anything left.’ Siliya handed him the remaining four annas. Jingling them, Payag said, ‘You've made me rich today! Rukmin used to put me off with a few pice.’ ‘I'm not going to bury it. Is money for food and drink or for burying?’ ‘From now on you go to market. Rukmin will do the housework.’ War broke out between Rukmin and Siliya. To retain her hold over Payag, Siliya worked herself to death. Her mill could be heard hours before dawn. At daybreak she went for grass, rested briefly and set off for market. Even on returning she did not sit idle: she spun hemp or gathered firewood. Rukmin continually found fault with her management and, whenever possible, collected dung, made cakes and sold them in the village. Payag had sweets in both hands. Both women tried to win him by giving him more money and the greater share of affection. But Siliya had established herself so firmly that nothing could shake her. At last the rivals openly clashed. One day Siliya returned with grass, drenched in sweat. It was Phagun, and the sunshine was strong. She thought she would bathe before going to market, left the grass at the door and went to the pond. Rukmin removed a little grass, hid it at a neighbor's and loosened the bundle to make it look the same size. Returning, Siliya thought it smaller and asked Rukmin. ‘I don't know,’ she said. Siliya began cursing: ‘May worms fill whoever touched my grass! May her father and brother die! May her eyes burst!’ For a while Rukmin restrained herself, but at last her blood boiled. She rose angrily and slapped Siliya two or three times. Siliya beat her chest and wept. The whole neighborhood gathered. Her good sense and industry offended them all: why did she cut the most grass, bring the most firewood, rise so early and earn so much? These things deprived her of their sympathy. They all began abusing her. Such an uproar for a handful of grass! A person shakes off and throws away that much. Was it grass or gold? Even if someone took it, she should remember it was someone of her own village and home, not an outside thief. Whom had she cursed so much? Her own neighbors! By chance Payag had gone to the police station that day. Returning exhausted in the evening, he told Siliya, ‘Give me some money so I can have a smoke. I'm worn out.’ At the sight of him she began wailing. Alarmed, he asked, ‘What happened? Why are you crying? Has someone died? Has anyone come from your parents' home?’ ‘I won't stay in this house now. I'll go home.’ ‘Say something! What happened? Has someone in the village abused you? Who? I'll burn his house, have him prosecuted.’ Weeping, Siliya told the whole story. Payag had been badly beaten at the station and was irritable. Hearing her tale set his body on fire. Rukmin had gone to fetch water. Before she could even set down the pitcher, he fell on her and beat her senseless. She answered blows with curses, and every curse enraged him into beating her harder, until her knees were split and her bangles broken. Siliya kept interjecting, ‘What impudence! What a tongue! I've never seen such a woman. Woman? A witch! No restraint on her mouth at all!’ But Rukmin seemed not to hear. All her strength went into cursing Payag. He tired of beating, but her tongue did not tire. She kept repeating, ‘Die! May your funeral be carried out! May Bhavani devour you! May you have seizures!’ Again and again Payag writhed with rage and came to kick her several times. But perhaps she no longer felt the blows. She did not even move, sitting on the ground with loose hair and chanting these spells. Her voice now held no anger, only a frenzied flow. Her whole soul blazed with the wish for destruction. At dark Rukmin rose and went off to one side, like a stream of tears leaving the eyes. Siliya was cooking. She saw her go but asked nothing. Payag sat smoking at the door; he too said nothing. When the crops began ripening, Payag had to watch the fields for a month and a half or two. Farmers paid him a fixed amount of grain per plow at both harvests. In Magh he cleared a little space amid the fields and built a thatched hut. After eating at night, he took fire, pipe, tobacco and charas there and lay down. This continued until the end of Chait. Those were the days now. The crop stood ripe and ready; harvesting would begin in a few days. Payag waited for Rukmin until ten that night. Then, supposing she was asleep at a neighbor's, he ate, picked up his staff and told Siliya, ‘Shut the door. If Rukmin comes, open it, coax her and give her a little food. What a storm there was over you today! I don't know how I grew so angry. I'd never touched her even with a flower's stem. If she's drowned herself somewhere, we'll be in trouble tomorrow.’ Siliya said, ‘Who knows whether she'll come? How can I stay alone? I'm afraid.’ ‘Then who will stay in the house? Someone may find it empty and carry off a pot or plate. What is there to fear? Rukmin will surely come.’ Siliya closed the reed door from inside. Payag set off for the fields. High on charas, he sang this devotional song: ‘Deceiver, why do your eyes flash so? Cut a pumpkin for a drum, a lemon for cymbals; five ridge gourds sing wedding songs, darling cucumber dances. Wearing silver you show your beauty, wearing gold you charm; a tulsi-bead necklace round your neck, you bewilder all three worlds. Deceiver—’ Suddenly, on reaching the village boundary, he saw that someone had lit a fire in the fields ahead. In a moment a blaze flared up. He shouted, ‘Who's there? Hey, who's lighting that fire?’ The rising flames answered with their fiery tongues. Now Payag realized his hut was burning. His heart pounded. Setting that hut alight was like setting fire to a heap of cotton. Wind was blowing. Only a cubit from the hut, ripe crops lay like a spread sheet on every side. Even at night their golden color showed. One tongue of flame, one tiny spark, would reduce the entire expanse to ashes. The whole village would be ruined. Fields of other villages adjoined these; they too would burn. Oh, the flames were rising! There was no time to delay. He threw down his dung cake and pipe, put his iron-bound staff on his shoulder and ran wildly toward the hut. The embankment route was roundabout, so he ran straight across the fields. Every moment the blaze grew fiercer and his feet moved faster. Even a swift horse could not have caught him then. He marveled at his own speed. His feet seemed scarcely to touch the earth. His eyes were fixed on the hut; he saw nothing to right or left. That concentration gave wings to his feet. Neither breath nor legs failed him. He covered three or four furlongs in two minutes and reached the hut. No one was near it. Who had done this? [Source page marker: 1405.] There was no time to think, much less to search. Payag suspected Rukmin, but this was no time for anger. Like mischievous children, the flames laughed, jostled and darted now right, now left. It seemed they would reach the crop at any instant, insistently advancing toward the plots, failing and leaping again with doubled force. How could the fire be extinguished? Beating it with a staff offered no chance; that would be sheer folly. What then? If the crops burned, he could never show his face to anyone. Ah, the village would be filled with lamentation. All would be destroyed. He did not think long. Rustics do not know how to think. Gripping his staff, he leaped into the fire at the hut's entrance, lifted the burning hut on his staff and ran toward the village along the widest embankment, carrying it above his head. It looked like a fiery vehicle flying through the air. Burning scraps of straw fell on him, but he was unaware. Once a clump broke away and fell on his hand, roasting it entirely. Yet his feet did not stop for an instant, nor did his hands hesitate. A movement of the hands meant the crops' destruction. There was no uncertainty now about Payag. The only fear was that the hut's central part, where he had inserted the staff's knob to lift it, might burn through. As soon as the hole widened, the hut would fall over him and engulf him in a fiery tomb. He knew this and flew with the wind's speed. The run was four furlongs. Death in the shape of fire played over his head and over the village crop. He ran so fast that the flames turned backward, most of their burning power spent struggling with the wind. Otherwise the fire would already have reached the middle and cries of disaster would have arisen. One furlong passed, and his courage did not fail. The second too was completed. Take care, Payag, two furlongs remain. Don't let your feet slacken at all. Once the flame reaches the staff's knob, your life is over. Even after death you will be cursed; for eternity you will burn in the fire of people's sighs. [Source page marker: 1406: Mansarovar.] Only one minute more! Only two fields remain now. Disaster! The staff's knob has slipped out. The hut is sliding down. No hope remains. Payag is running his very life out. The field at the edge has arrived. Only two seconds more! Twenty cubits ahead the gate of victory stands welcoming him. There is heaven; here is hell. But the sliding hut has reached his head. He could still throw it down and save himself, but he has no attachment to life. He runs on with that blazing fire over his head. There his feet stagger! This cruel play of fire can no longer be watched. Suddenly a woman ran from beneath the tree ahead and reached him. It was Rukmin. At once she stepped before him, bent her neck, went beneath the burning hut and took it on both hands. That same instant Payag fell unconscious. His whole face was scorched. Carrying his blaze, Rukmin reached the field boundary in a second, but in that short distance her hands and face burned and her clothes caught fire. She no longer had the awareness even to step out from under the hut. She fell carrying it. For a while the hut moved as she flung her arms and legs; then fire swallowed her. Rukmin entered her fiery tomb. After a while Payag regained consciousness. His whole body burned. He saw red fire glowing in the straw beneath the tree, rose and ran to it, and pushed the fire aside with his foot. Beneath lay Rukmin's half-burned body. He sat, covered his face with both hands and wept. In the morning villagers carried him home. He was treated for a week but did not survive. Fire had burned part of him; the fire of grief completed what remained.
