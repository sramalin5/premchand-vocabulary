'अच्छा, तो माँगती हूँ। मुझे अपने साथ होली खेलने दो।' पंडित जी का रंग उड़ गया। आँखें फाड़ कर बोले —  होली खेलने दूँ ? मैं तो होली खेलता नहीं। कभी नहीं खेला। होली खेलना होता, तो घर में छिप कर क्यों बैठता। 'और के साथ मत खेलो; लेकिन मेरे साथ तो खेलना ही पड़ेगा।' 'यह मेरे नियम के विरुद्ध है। जिस चीज को अपने घर में उचित समझूँ उसे किस न्याय से घर के बाहर अनुचित समझूँ, सोचो।' चम्पा ने सिर नीचा करके कहा —घर में ऐसी कितनी बातें उचित समझते हो, जो घर के बाहर करना अनुचित ही नहीं पाप भी है। पंडित जी झेंपते हुए बोले —  अच्छा भाई, तुम जीती, मैं हारा। अब मैं तुम से यही दान माँगता हूँ... 'पहले मेरा पुरस्कार दे दो, पीछे मुझसे दान माँगना' , यह कहते हुए चम्पा ने लोटे का रंग उठा लिया और पंडित जी को सिर से पाँव तक नहला दिया। जब तक वह उठ कर भागें उसने मुट्ठी भर गुलाल ले कर सारे मुँह में पोत दिया। पंडित जी रोनी सूरत बना कर बोले—  अभी और कसर बाकी हो, तो वह भी पूरी कर लो। मैं जानता था कि तुम मेरी आस्तीन का साँप बनोगी। अब और कुछ रंग बाकी नहीं रहा ? चम्पा ने पति के मुख की ओर देखा, तो उस पर मनोवेदना का गहरा रंग झलक रहा था। पछता कर बोली—  क्या तुम सचमुच बुरा मान गये हो ? मैं तो समझती थी कि तुम केवल मुझे चिढ़ा रहे हो। श्रीविलास ने काँपते हुए स्वर में कहा— नहीं चम्पा, मुझे बुरा नहीं लगा। हाँ, तुमने मुझे उस कर्तव्य की याद दिला दी, जो मैं अपनी कायरता के कारण भुला बैठा था। वह सामने जो चित्र देख रही हो, मेरे परम मित्र मनहरनाथ का है, जो अब संसार में नहीं है। तुमसे क्या कहूँ, कितना सरस, कितना भावुक, कितना साहसी आदमी था ! देश की दशा देख-देख कर उसका खून जलता रहता था। ह्म भी कोई उम्र होती है, पर वह उसी उम्र में अपने जीवन का मार्ग निश्चित कर चुका था। सेवा करने का अवसर पा कर वह इस तरह उसे पकड़ता था, मानो सम्पत्ति हो। जन्म का विरागी था। वासना तो उसे छू ही न गयी थी। हमारे और साथी सैर-सपाटे करते थे; पर उसका मार्ग सबसे अलग था। सत्य के लिए प्राण देने को तैयार, कहीं अन्याय देखा और भवें तन गयीं, कहीं पत्रों में अत्याचार की खबर देखी और चेहरा तमतमा उठा। ऐसा तो मैंने आदमी ही नहीं देखा। ईश्वर ने अकाल ही बुला लिया, नहीं तो वह मनुष्यों में रत्न होता। किसी मुसीबत के मारे का उद्धार करने को अपने प्राण हथेली पर लिये फिरता था। स्त्री-जाति का इतना आदर और सम्मान कोई क्या करेगा ? स्त्री उसके लिए पूजा और भक्ति की वस्तु थी। पाँच वर्ष हुए, यही होली का दिन था। मैं भंग के नशे में चूर, रंग में सिर से पाँव तक नहाया हुआ, उसे गाना सुनने के लिए बुलाने गया, तो देखा कि वह कपड़े पहने कहीं जाने को तैयार है। पूछा—कहाँ जा रहे हो ? उसने मेरा हाथ पकड़ कर कहा —तुम अच्छे वक्त पर आ गये, नहीं तो मुझे जाना पड़ता। एक अनाथ बुढ़िया मर गयी है, कोई उसे कंधा देनेवाला नहीं मिलता। कोई किसी मित्र से मिलने गया हुआ है, कोई नशे में चूर पड़ा हुआ है, कोई मित्रों की दावत कर रहा है, कोई महफिल सजाये बैठा है। कोई लाश को उठानेवाला नहीं। ब्राह्मण-क्षत्री उस चमारिन की लाश कैसे छुएँगे, उनका तो धर्म भ्रष्ट होता है, कोई तैयार नहीं होता ! बड़ी मुश्किल से दो कहार मिले हैं। एक मैं हूँ, चौथे आदमी की कमी थी, सो ईश्वर ने तुम्हें भेज दिया। चलो, चलें। हाय ! अगर मैं जानता कि यह प्यारे मनहर का आदेश है, तो आज मेरी आत्मा को इतनी ग्लानि न होती। मेरे घर कई मित्र आये हुए थे। गाना हो रहा था। उस वक्त लाश उठा कर नदी जाना मुझे अप्रिय लगा। बोला —  इस वक्त तो भाई, मैं नहीं जा सकूँगा। घर पर मेहमान बैठे हुए हैं। मैं तुम्हें बुलाने आया था। मनहर ने मेरी ओर तिरस्कार के नेत्रों से देख कर कहा —अच्छी बात है, तुम जाओ; मैं और कोई साथी खोज लूँगा। मगर तुमसे मुझे ऐसी आशा नहीं थी। तुमने भी वही कहा, जो तुमसे पहले औरों ने कहा था। कोई नयी बात नहीं थी। अगर हम लोग अपने कर्तव्य को भूल न गये होते, तो आज यह दशा ही क्यों होती ? ऐसी होली को धिक्कार है ! त्योहार, तमाशा देखने, अच्छी-अच्छी चीजें खाने और अच्छे-अच्छे कपड़े पहनने का नाम नहीं है। यह व्रत है, तप है, अपने भाइयों से प्रेम और सहानुभूति करना ही त्योहार का खास मतलब है और कपड़े लाल करने के पहले खून को लाल कर लो। सफेद खून पर यह लाली शोभा नहीं देती। यह कह कर वह चला गया। मुझे उस वक्त यह फटकारें बहुत बुरी मालूम हुईं। अगर मुझमें वह सेवा-भाव न था, तो उसे मुझे यों धिक्कारने का कोई अधिकार न था। घर चला आया; If you won't touch it, how can I?’ Pleased, Silbil said, ‘Undoubtedly, that is a virtuous wife's duty.’ ‘But my brothers are coming. Why would they agree?’ ‘I have thought of a plan for them too. Making it succeed is your job. I'll pretend to be ill, cover myself with a sheet and lie down. Tell them I have a fever. There: we're free.’ Rolling her eyes, she said, ‘Heaven forbid! What things you say! Let fever go to an enemy's house; if the wretch comes here, I'll scorch its face.’ ‘What other plan is there?’ ‘Hide in the little room upstairs. I'll say you've taken a purgative and going out would expose you to the air.’ The Pandit brightened. ‘Yes, yes, that's best.’ [Source marker: 1389.] It is Holi. Outside there is an uproar. In old days people played only with abir and gulal; now blue, green and black have all joined forces, and no human can escape this alliance. Gods perhaps can. Silbil's two brothers-in-law were targets for all the neighbourhood's men, women, children and old people. The outer sitting room's floor, walls and even pictures had been coloured. Inside it was the same. Would the neighbourhood's sisters-in-law ever hold back? Even the drain ran coloured. The elder brother asked, ‘Champa, is he really unwell? He hasn't even come to eat.’ With lowered head Champa said, ‘Yes, brother, his stomach began hurting last night. The doctor has forbidden him to go out into the air.’ A little later the younger asked, ‘Sister, won't brother-in-law come down? What sort of illness is this? Shall I go up and see him?’ Catching his hand, Champa said, ‘No, no, don't go up! He won't play with colours. The doctor has forbidden him to go out into the air.’ Both brothers could only wring their hands. Suddenly the younger had an idea: why not play Holi with brother-in-law's clothes? They weren't ill! The elder liked the idea. What could their poor sister do? The Alexanders took the keys from her and coloured every garment Silbil owned, not sparing even a handkerchief. When Champa hung them on the courtyard clothesline, it looked as though a dyer had coloured wedding outfits. Silbil watched from upstairs but said nothing. A snake seemed to writhe upon his breast. Every garment ruined, nothing left even for the office! What quarrel did these villains have with his clothes? All sorts of delicious dishes were being made. Champa worked alongside a Brahmin woman from the neighbourhood. When the two brothers and several other gentlemen sat to eat in the courtyard, the elder asked Champa, ‘Have you made some khichri for him [source marker: 1390 : मानसरोवर] too? The poor fellow won't be able to eat puris today.’ Champa said, ‘Not yet. I'll make it now.’ ‘What brains you have! You haven't even thought what the poor man will eat. You were never so careless. Go and fetch rice and mung lentils quickly.’ So khichri began cooking while the friends began their meal. Upstairs Silbil lamented his fate. He saw only one cause of this whole calamity: marriage! Had Champa not come, why would these brothers-in-law have come, why would his clothes have been ruined, why would he be given mung khichri on Holi? But what use was regret now? By the time the others finished, the khichri was ready. The elder brother himself sent Champa upstairs with a plate. Silbil glared at it. ‘Take it away from me.’ ‘Will you fast today?’ ‘If that is your wish, so be it.’ ‘What have I done? I've been working since morning. My brother himself had the khichri put on and sent me here.’ ‘Yes, I can see I am not master of this house. The Alexanders have occupied it. But I refuse to believe that, had you wished, my plate couldn't have arrived before everyone else's. I consider this contrary to a devoted wife's duty; what else can I say?’ ‘You saw how both of them were riding on my head.’ ‘A fine joke: others feast on samosas and flaky pastries while I am given mung khichri. What luck!’ ‘Eat a few mouthfuls. As soon as I get a chance, I'll bring another plate.’ ‘You had all my clothes coloured. How shall I go to the office? I don't like this joking at all. I call it hooliganism. Why did you give them the trunk key? May I ask that much?’ [Source marker: 1391.] ‘They snatched it by force. Didn't you hear? What could I do?’ ‘Well, what's done is done. Take this plate away. Bring another if you consider it your duty; otherwise let today be a fast.’ Suddenly hearing footsteps, Silbil looked up and saw both brothers-in-law approaching. The poor man pulled a face, covered himself with the sheet and began groaning. The elder said, ‘How are you feeling? Eat a little khichri.’ Making a face, Silbil said, ‘I don't feel like eating just now.’ ‘No, fasting will be harmful. Eat it.’ Inwardly cursing both devils, poor Silbil swallowed the khichri like poison. Khichri had been written in his destiny for Holi! The two remained until it was all finished, like jail officials feeding a prisoner on hunger strike. The poor man had to stuff himself until there was no room left for delicacies. At ten that night Champa came to her husband with a platter of excellent food. He was inwardly fuming: who cares for me before her brothers? Where did these two devils spring from? They made me fast all day, and still no sign of dinner. At last the sight of Champa's platter cooled the fire a little. ‘It's still very early,’ he said. ‘Why didn't you come an hour or two later?’ Setting it down, Champa said, ‘You will accept neither defeat nor victory. After all, two guests have come; I must look after them. You yourself would dislike it otherwise. They won't come every day.’ ‘God forbid they should! One day has already finished me.’ Suddenly, seeing the fragrant, rich dishes, a rosy smile spread across the Pandit's lotus face [source marker as printed: 139ह्म : मानसरोवर]. He praised Champa with every dish he ate. ‘Truly, Champa, I've never eaten such things. What could a confectioner make beside this? I feel like giving you a reward.’ ‘You're teasing me. What can I do? I made what I know how to make.’ ‘No, truly. My very soul is satisfied. Today I've learned food concerns the soul more than the stomach. Tell me, what reward shall I give?’ ‘Will you give whatever I ask?’ ‘I will—I swear by my sacred thread!’ ‘If you don't, my word means nothing.’ ‘I tell you I will. How else can I say it? Shall I put it in writing?’ ‘Very well, then: let me play Holi with you.’ The Pandit turned pale. Eyes wide, he said, ‘Let you play Holi? I don't play it. I never have. If I wanted to play, why would I hide indoors?’ ‘Don't play with others, but you must play with me.’ ‘That is against my principles. Think: by what reasoning could I consider something proper at home and improper outside?’ Lowering her head, Champa said, ‘You consider many things proper at home which would be not merely improper but sinful outside.’ Embarrassed, he said, ‘All right, you win, I lose. Now I ask this boon of you—’ ‘Give me my reward first; ask your boon afterwards.’ Saying this, Champa lifted the pot of coloured water and drenched him from head to foot. Before he could rise and flee, she took a handful of gulal and smeared it over his face. Looking tearful, he said, ‘If anything is still lacking, finish that too. I knew you would become a snake in my sleeve. Is there no more colour left?’ Champa looked at him and saw deep pain colouring his face. Regretfully she said, ‘Have you really taken offence? I thought you were only teasing me.’ In a trembling voice Shrivilas said, ‘No, Champa, I haven't taken offence. But you have reminded me of the duty my cowardice made me forget. That picture before you is of my dearest friend Manharnath, who is no longer alive. What can I tell you? What a sensitive, feeling, courageous man he was! Seeing the country's condition made his blood burn. At so young an age—the printed number is unclear—he had already chosen his life's path. He seized any chance to serve as though it were wealth. He was detached from birth; desire had never touched him. Our other friends went on outings, but his path was apart. Ready to die for truth, he frowned at injustice and flushed at reports of oppression in the papers. I have never seen such a man. God called him too soon; otherwise he would have been a jewel among men. He went about with his life in his hands to rescue the afflicted. Who could honour and respect women more? To him a woman was an object of worship and devotion. Five years ago, on this very Holi day, I was intoxicated with cannabis and drenched in colour from head to foot. I went to invite him to hear music, and found him dressed and ready to go somewhere. “Where are you going?” I asked. Taking my hand, he said, “You have come at the right moment; otherwise I would have had to leave. A helpless old woman has died, and nobody can be found to carry her bier. One has gone to visit a friend, another lies drunk, another is giving a feast, another has arranged a musical gathering. Nobody will lift the corpse. How could Brahmins and Kshatriyas touch a Chamar woman's body? Their religion would be defiled! Nobody agrees. With great difficulty I found two bearers. I am the third; we needed a fourth, and God has sent you. Come, let us go.” Alas, had I known this was dear Manhar's command, my soul would not feel such remorse today. Several friends were at my house; music was being performed. Taking a corpse to the river then displeased me. I said, “Brother, I can't go now. Guests are sitting at home. I came to invite you.” Manhar looked at me contemptuously. “Very well, go. I'll find someone else. But I didn't expect this of you. You've said exactly what everyone before you said; nothing new. Had we not forgotten our duty, why would things be like this today? Shame on such a Holi! A festival doesn't mean watching amusements, eating delicacies and wearing fine clothes. It is a vow, a discipline. Love and sympathy for our fellows are its true meaning. Before colouring your clothes red, make your blood red. This redness does not suit blood turned white.” He left. His rebukes offended me greatly at the time. If I lacked his spirit of service, he had no right to condemn me like that. I returned home, but his words kept echoing in my ears. All Holi's pleasure was spoiled. We did not meet for a month. College had closed for examination preparation, so we did not meet there either. I knew nothing of when or how he fell ill or went home. Suddenly one day I received a letter from him. Ah, even today reading it makes my breast seem to burst.’ For a moment Shrivilas could not speak; his throat had closed. Then he continued, ‘I shall show it to you another day. He wrote, “Come and see me one last time. Perhaps we shall not meet again in this life.” The letter fell from my hand. His home was in Meerut district. Another train left in half an hour. I set out immediately, but I was not destined to see him: he had passed away before I arrived. Champa, after that I never played Holi; I gave up every other festival too. Perhaps God did not give me the power to act. Now I long for someone to give me work in service. I cannot step forward myself, but am ready to follow. Yet nobody gives me work either. Today, by throwing that colour, you reminded me of his rebuke. May God give me strength to become Manhar in action as well as thought.’ Saying this, Shrivilas took gulal from the tray, sprinkled it upon the picture and bowed.
