¶1 It is a mistake to say husband and wife must have matching temperaments for marital happiness. Mrs Gaura and Kunwar Ratan Singh agreed in nothing. Gaura was generous; Ratan Singh gripped every cowrie between his teeth. She was cheerful, he contemplative. She prized family honour above life; he considered it pretence. Their social conduct and ideas differed greatly too, though here Ratan Singh held the palm for liberality. Gaura objected to communal dining, detested widow remarriage and opposed the question of untouchables; he supported all these reforms. Politically the difference was more complicated. She considered the existing order immovable, eternal and unavoidable, and kept aloof from moderates, militants, Congress, self-rule and Home Rule alike. ‘What can this handful of educated men achieve? Can chickpeas burst the roasting oven?’ she asked. He was a determined optimist, in the front row at political meetings and first to act, vowed to Indian-made goods and wholly committed to boycott. Despite these differences they were happy. Disagreements arose, but like breezes adorning still water with gentle ripples, not storms turning the sea into turmoil. Coverage and translation notes

Suhaag and textile wordplay
Suhaag means more than a wedding: it carries the auspiciousness of a woman's married life while her husband lives. The title and recurring wedding-sari image are explained in that sense. The source's playful fabric-name changes in ¶2 are conveyed through pleasure and pain rather than inventing English textile names.
Source chronology and social language
Ram Tahal's timing of the departure relative to the bonfire in ¶71 conflicts with the earlier sequence; his account is retained without silently correcting it. His caste comparison and Kesar's figurative widowhood are their own words, reflecting how occupation and secure livelihood shape their social dignity. All closing proposals are included.
