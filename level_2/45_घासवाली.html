¶16 Under the neem tree's shade at the doorway, Mahavir stood rubbing down his horse. He saw Mulia going out with a tearful face but could say nothing. Had he been able, he would have sheltered her within his heart, hidden her in his eyes. But the horse had to be fed. Bought grass would cost no less than twelve annas a day. Coverage and translation notes

Caste and consent
Chamar and Thakur identify the unequal social positions central to Mulia's argument. The assertion in ¶10 that lower-caste beauty exists for upper-caste men's pleasure expresses the predatory assumption the narrative exposes. Mulia rejects it explicitly.
Coins and livelihoods
A rupee then comprised sixteen annas; pice were smaller units. The translation retains these money terms so the detailed comparison of earnings and fodder costs remains visible.
The final exchange
Chain Singh's emphatic claim in ¶90 is retained even though ¶67 described his earlier impressions differently. The story leaves that tension in his words and thoughts intact.
